# Napoleon's Triumph UI interaction visual contract

## Visual intents

- **Board view.** The board is drawn north up, or turned a quarter so one army's side of the table is at the bottom of the screen. A wide screen opens on the viewer's own lines; a tall one opens north up. The map menu at the bottom right corner of the board steps through the three views. Stacks in reserve are laid out to read upright in whichever view is shown; commander flags and count badges stay upright.
- **Standing clear.** Pieces in reserve gather on their locale's anchor. When pieces already placed lie in the way there, most often a corps blocking one of the locale's own approaches, the whole reserve stands aside to the clearest place inside its locale, as near the anchor as it can. Blocking pieces keep to their approach, but on a wide approach they take whichever half lies across less of what is already placed. Where a locale has no clear room, stacks may still overlap.
- **Fronting an approach.** Pieces blocking an approach line up along it, inside their locale, and face across it. Each group (a corps, or the detached units there) stands in single file; a wide approach holds two files, so two groups front it side by side and a lone group leaves half of it open, while further groups fall in behind the shorter file. In each file the top of each block's face points at the approach, and the block at the approach lies whole on top with the ranks behind it tucked under. This holds in every board view, so a block facing down the screen shows its face upside down.
- **Held approaches.** The bar of an approach with pieces blocking it is tinted in their army's colour on their locale's side, for both players, in History View and for the army as arranged in set-up, so two armies facing each other across one approach each colour their own half.
- **Commander flags.** A corps's flag stands on the top of its stack as the board is turned, so the banner clears its own blocks at any angle: at the highest point by choice, at the other end of the top block when that covers less. It flies to the right unless flying left covers less. What it avoids, in order of weight, is the arrows (the attack marker and any threat arrows on offer), then other stacks and flags already placed. The banner is always opaque.
- **Map menu.** The board's own controls sit at its bottom right corner: a shortcut to the viewer's own command track, the two map aids and the board view. They are light paper chips like the map legend, never dark; a map aid shows whether it is on by a small ring that fills. On a wide screen they are always shown; on a phone they fold behind one *Map* button so they do not cover the map.
- **Map aids.** Two switches in the map menu, both off to begin with. *Darker borders* is the player's own preference, saved to their account and the same in every game of the title; *Approach symbols* is kept for the session. *Darker borders* lays a light tint over every printed approach bar, impassable ones included, so locales are easier to tell apart; it stops at the edges of the title, the order-of-battle strips and the tracks. *Approach symbols* draws each side's printed marks (infantry, cavalry and artillery penalties, obstruction, impassable) beside its bar, in the printed order and proportions but larger and over the pieces, for when pieces cover the printed ones.
- **Command tracks.** A black disc lies on a commander's box of the printed command track once that commander has given a command this turn, and on one Independent box for each independent command used. Both armies' tracks show for both players and in History View; the discs go when the turn ends. The tracks are printed along one edge of the board, so a turned board resting at its covering fit leaves the viewer's own track out of sight: *Command track* in the map menu brings the view to it.
- **Morale and time tracks.** Each army's morale is a disc in its own colour on the printed Morale Track, and the round is a black disc on the printed Time Track. A disc lies in the half of its box away from the printed number or hour, so the print stays readable under it; two armies at one level lie side by side in that half. Before play begins the time disc is on the scenario's start box, and when the game is over it stays on the last round played; an army has no morale disc before the armies are chosen or once it has broken. Both show for both players and in History View.
- **Choosing pieces.** On their turn a player taps a corps or a group of detached units on the map. The group is ringed, its orders open in the action area, and every place it can go is marked on the map: locales to move into (road marches in a second tint), approaches to block, and arrows for approaches it can threaten. A corps is chosen whole: its units sit in the unit strip inside one outline, none marked on its own, and the command is a Corps Move. Tapping units, in the strip or on the corps's own blocks on the map, chooses the ones to send instead; both drive the same choice: each is outlined, the blocks left out are dimmed on the map, and the command switches to a Unit Move for one unit or a Detach Move for several, with the Corps Move still on offer. Choosing every unit, or none, is the whole corps again. In a group of detached units one unit is chosen at a time: the block tapped is the one picked up, tapping another switches to it, and tapping the chosen one puts it down. The exception is two artillery units blocking a wide approach, which may attack across it together, each by its own Unit Move: tapping the second adds it to the first, the switch stays on Unit Move, a line says the two fire together, and the only place marked is the threat across their approach. Two artillery units chosen out of a corps on a wide approach are offered the same Unit Move beside the Detach Move, when the corps keeps another unit. Announcing a Guard Attack is offered only when the chosen units include Guard infantry and can threaten across an approach that is not obstructed; once announced, only those threats stay marked. Tapping empty map puts the pieces down. A threat arrow lies over the pieces that can make the threat, so a tap that falls on one of the chosen group's own blocks under an arrow chooses the unit, and the arrow takes the tap everywhere else. When the pieces picked up would lie under the orders sheet, the board is moved, at the zoom it has, to bring them out from under it.
- **Units already moved.** While no attack is on, a player sees a dot at the end of each of their own blocks that has moved this turn; in their command phase the lettering of a commander's flag is muted once the commander has given a command.
- **Commands left.** In the command phase the action area always says what the army may still command, in the rule's own terms: for the Allies the corps commands left of their five, for the French that there is no limit beyond one per corps, and for both the independent commands left. The armies panel gives the commands used, with the same limits.
- **Command switch.** Under the unit strip, and in an attack under the attacker's pieces, one three-way switch names the command by its rulebook name: Corps Move, Detach Move, Unit Move, always in that order. In an attack it sits under a small "Command" label, and across a wide approach the width is a second switch of the same kind, Wide or Narrow, under an "Attack width" label. The command in force is filled, and the thumb slides to it when it changes; commands the chosen units cannot be given are greyed and do not respond. It is never plain text, so a whole corps shows Corps Move on the switch with the other two greyed until units are tapped.
- **Map legend.** While pieces are chosen, a small key lies on the top edge of the map, just under the action area, naming the marks now on the map: move, march by road, block an approach, threaten an attack. Each entry shows the mark as it is drawn on the board, on a patch of the map's tone, and only marks actually on the map are listed, so an arrow in the key means there is an arrow to tap. The key is a light paper chip with rounded corners and its content centred in even padding, never dark and never the page's colour; it takes no taps, and lets taps through to the map beneath it. Threat arrows also carry a tooltip.
- **Armies panel.** Each corps lists its units as blocks in a tight grid, three to a row, with no tile chrome; they are for reading, not for tapping.
- **Finding a corps.** In the armies panel the location of every corps on the map, of either army, is a button with a target mark. Pressing it centres the board on that corps's locale and its neighbours; on a phone the table also scrolls from the panel to the board. It changes the view only, never the selection, and counts as moving the view by hand.
- **Riding on.** Cavalry that has taken a locale by road and may still ride on stays picked up without being asked: the action area says it is riding on, the riding units cannot be left out of the ride, and the map marks only what the same command still allows, which is locales and approaches along its road and arrows for the approaches it could threaten across. Picking up other pieces and commanding them ends the ride. The ride belongs to the units that ride, not to the group they stand in: when the cavalry has ridden into a locale where other detached units stand, those are in its group and its unit strip, left out of the ride, and tapping one that can still be commanded chooses it for a command of its own, as in any group of detached units. Tapping the cavalry, or the chosen unit again, takes the ride up again. The cavalry is not marked as moved in the strip while its ride is open. Cavalry that can still take a command of its own, which is French reinforcement cavalry in its turn of entry, gets a two-way switch beside the command switch, *Riding on* and *New command*, in the same sliding style. *Riding on* is in force unasked; *New command* offers the commands and marks the places of an ordinary pick-up, a line says that it ends the ride and is the second command of the turn of entry, and Undo returns to the ride. Riders in a corps can then be chosen among like the units of any corps. The same switch stands above the command switch in an attack wherever the riders named could attack either way, starting from what the player had chosen when threatening: at the feint and when following up a retreat. In the declaration of a pressed attack there is no switch, because an attack by road must be a feint: the riders are listed with the pieces in reserve and attack by a new command. Once the cavalry has used its road allowance, or a corps has had to halt beside an enemy corps, it stays picked up only to end blocking an approach of its locale that its road crosses; no locale and no threat is marked then.
- **Choosing the road.** A place that can be reached by more than one road is still marked once. When the roads leave different things behind for the rest of the turn (rule 10: a corps of two or more units closes the reserves it marches into to other units, and a reinforcement goes on only by roads joined to the one it came on by), tapping the place does not move the pieces yet: the place is outlined as chosen, the other marks go, and the map marks where the roads part, which is the locales to march through or, for roads that come on in one locale, the points on the map edge where they enter. A line in the action area says why. A tap on one of those marks makes the move; where the roads still part after it, the next parting is marked, and Undo takes the taps back one at a time before it takes back the place. Roads that leave the same behind are one road, the shortest standing for them, so a single unit already on the map is never asked. A place that can be gone to on foot is offered on foot only.
- **Reinforcements.** Corps still off the map are picked up from the action area; their entry locales and road marches are then marked like any other move. Units a corps left off the map when it came on are offered beside them as *Units left behind* and come on one at a time, each by a Unit Move.
- **Battle focus.** While an attack is being fought the view closes in on the two locales and returns to where it was when the attack is over. The view glides there when an action just played begins or ends the attack; on Undo, when stepping through the history, and when a position is restored it cuts, with no motion. Panning or zooming by hand cancels the return. Pieces in the two locales spread to full spacing so each block can be tapped. Every piece that has no part in the attack so far and cannot be picked for it is dimmed.
- **Pieces that cannot be picked.** In a step where some of the pieces shown may not be picked under the rules (foot in a counter-attack while the defence is holding, artillery always), they stay in the panel but disabled, painted in the washed-out tone, and cannot be tapped on the map. Tiles that are only there to be read (the forces named so far) are painted at full strength.
- **Naming pieces in an attack.** During the defence declaration, the feint decision, the attack declaration, the counter-attack, the loss assignment and the forced advance, the acting player taps blocks on the map or the matching tiles in the panel; both drive the same pick. A named block carries a letter at its end and stays in its stack: D for a defender, A for an attacker, C for a counter-attacker, and L for a leading unit of either side, since whose block it is already says which. The letter turns with the block. A unit picked in a step with no letter of its own (the advance, a retreat by itself) carries a plain lozenge. The panel's tiles carry the same letter on the piece and spell the role out in a word underneath. The moved dots are hidden during an attack, so the two never share a block. When the defence stands, the attacker is asked one thing: press the attack or call it a feint. No pieces or command are offered then, because a pressed attack names them only after the defence's leading units are shown (rule 11, steps 4 and 5). Calling it a feint opens the naming of the feinting pieces and where they end, and Undo goes back to the question; when the attack cannot be pressed (an attack by road against a defence that stands) the naming opens at once. At the declaration the attacker's pick starts from the command and pieces that made the threat; once the attacker names other pieces, the command is the first one those pieces can take, not the one the threat was planned with. The game keeps those for the attacker alone, so the start is the same after a reload or on another device, and the prompt says so only when it is so. A forced advance with a single unit and nothing to choose is made by the game and never asked; with a single unit whose commander may stay or go, the unit is already named and only the commander is asked about. A road attack names its command like any other: one cavalry unit of a larger corps goes by Unit Move, with the Corps Move on the switch for taking its commander along. Two batteries on a wide approach combine by naming both to lead. When a narrow attack meets two leading units, the attacker taps the one it strikes, on the map or on its tile; the other loses its L, being no longer a leading unit (rule 11, step 5).
- **A commander in an advance.** When defending units step up to block the approach, their commander stays behind unless the player sends him: his *goes with them* button starts unpressed, and the units that step up are detached. He is shown as going, and cannot be held back, only when every unit standing with him advances.
- **Retreat.** Locales the retreat can reach are marked on the map and an arrow with a count shows where the units are going. Tapping a locale sends every unit there. To split them up the player chooses "Send separately" for a unit, in the panel or by tapping its block, and the next locale tapped takes only that unit; the button then reads "Now tap its locale". That choice is offered only when more than one unit retreats and there is more than one locale to go to. When there is not room for every unit, the panel says how many will be lost and every unit can be picked: a unit with no place reads "Save this unit", and saving it takes the place of one sent earlier, the latest choice always having first claim (rule 12: the choice is the retreating player's). No defence roles are shown.
- **Attack marker.** The approach under attack carries an arrow from the attack locale into the defence locale for as long as the attack lasts, for both players and in History View.
- **Set-up.** Every corps starts empty. The action area holds, top to bottom: one line saying what a tap does now; the units still to assign, one stack per kind of unit with its count; a row of seats, one per corps, set off by a slightly darker ground and no outline; and the Deploy button with what is still to do beside it. One seat is open at a time, shown by a darker ground, and a unit tapped in the pool goes to the open seat; tapping a unit in any seat sends it back to the pool. A seat shows a slot, tinted in the army's own colour, for each unit the corps still needs for its minimum. Once the minimum is met it shows one faint free slot, and another each time that one is filled, for as long as the corps has room: eight to a commander, less where its set-up locale holds less or units detached there take the room. A corps with no room shows no free slot, and while it is open the line at the top says which limit it has met: the eight units of a corps, what its locale holds, or the eight counting the units detached from it. A reinforcement corps (Bernadotte, Davout) keeps one needed slot, marked *not 2-str. infantry*, until it has a unit that is not two-strength infantry, and the line says so while it is open; with seven two-strength infantry and no other unit it is offered no eighth. *Detached* shows one free slot at a time the same way. Seats are only as tall as the fullest of them needs, so the map moves down a line when a corps outgrows the rest. A pool stack that the open seat cannot take is painted in the washed-out tone and cannot be tapped; a stack with no unit left is painted the same way with its count in a muted ink. On the map each corps that starts there has its set-up locale tinted in the army's colour, and tapping the locale or the corps opens it. The open seat is always marked on the map: an open corps has its locale tinted more strongly and outlined with the dashed line of the selection ring, and its pieces ringed; the fixed battery is ringed while its seat is open; with *Detached* open every detached unit is ringed, or only the one in hand while one is. A corps held off the map has nothing to mark. Before it has units a corps plants its flag in the locale, showing 0. The map shows the army as arranged so far, and nothing for units still in the pool. Where the open corps stands is chosen on the map: the approaches of its own locale are marked, and its reserve when it stands on an approach. The French have two more seats. *Detached* takes up to six units set up on their own: a unit tapped in the pool is taken in hand, the locales it may stand in are marked, and tapping one stands it there in reserve; it stays in hand, ringed on the map, with the approaches of that locale marked, so a second tap puts it on an approach. *Fixed battery* takes one artillery unit the same way, locale and then approach, and has to end on an approach; in the set-up locale of a corps it uses none of the six detachments. With the Santon rule in use the set-up starts with a battery already on the Santon, in reserve, as one of the six; it cannot be turned or taken back, and its seat only shows it. A unit standing on its own is taken in hand again by tapping it on the map. Which commander a detached unit is counted as coming from is never asked and never shown: a detached unit appears only in *Detached* (or *Fixed battery*), and every corps seat asks for its whole minimum for as long as the pool can fill every corps. Only when the pool cannot are detached units counted towards the corps they can have come from; such a corps then shows no needed slot below its minimum, and the line says so while it is open. On 1 December each Allied seat carries a switch to start on the map or hold off it, live for two corps; a corps that cannot start on the map says why in place of the switch: *two chosen*, or *too many units* for its locale. Deploy is live once every unit is placed and the rules accept the set-up; until then the line beside it counts the units to assign and then gives the rule still broken.
- **Set-up view.** A set-up opens with the view on the locales the army sets up in, so every corps can be found before any seat is tapped. On a small screen (narrow, or as short as a phone on its side), opening a seat closes the view in on it: on a corps's set-up locale, or the locale the fixed battery stands in, and the locales around it; a seat with no one place on the map (a corps held off it, *Detached*, a fixed battery not yet placed) brings the whole set-up area back, which is also where a unit taken in hand finds the locales it may stand in. Taking a unit that stands on its own in hand by tapping it on the map moves nothing: the view stays where the tap was. The view is aimed at the part of the map that is on screen, because there the panel is tall and the lower part of the map is below the fold. On a wide screen the view does not zoom: it stays as it is, and only if it has been moved off the open corps's locale is that brought back in, at the zoom the board has. When the set-up is deployed the whole field comes back.
- **Whole-field picking on small screens.** When pieces are picked up with the whole field in view, the view closes in on the pieces and the places they can go.
- **Header and action area.** One band across the top of the game column, set apart from the page by its ground alone, with no outline. It meets the page header above, and it and the board run to the window's right edge; only the side panel keeps a gap around it. In the band: a masthead line (which army's turn in italic, the time beside it at the same size, and Undo as plain italic text with an arrow standing on its baseline, never an outlined button; morale is not shown here but on the army cards in the side panel), a swelled rule with a lozenge, and under it the prompt and controls for the decision at hand. The masthead carries a light tint of the acting army's colour that fades out just below the rule; in History View and at the end of the game there is none. The masthead names the army whose turn it is (*Allies' turn*, *French turn*) for both players rather than *Your turn*, at the player's request: the tint and the army named already say whose it is, and both players read the same line. On a small screen the date beside the time is shortened (*1 Dec*).
- **The map keeps half the viewport on a small screen.** The board is never shorter than half the viewport's height on a small screen and two fifths of it on a wider one, or the whole space under the masthead where that is less, and the part of it the orders sheet hangs over does not count towards that. When a panel is too tall for the map to fit under it, the panel and the map scroll together under the masthead, which stays where it is and takes no part in the scrolling; the scrolling stops dead at either end, with no spring. A new step opens scrolled to its prompt.
- **History.** The history panel files what happened under the round it happened in. An attack is one entry: the threat opens it and every step of the attack (the defence or retreat, the feint or the pressed attack, the counter-attack, losses, regrouping, the advance, the move into the locale) is listed under it, indented, as it happens, each with the stripe of the army that acted. The next threat opens an entry of its own. Ends of turns are left out. A line also says what morale an army paid beyond its losses, as the points its morale really fell: committing the Guard or the heavy cavalry where that happened (the declaration, the counter-attack, a loss, a retreat), and the defeat of a Guard Attack after the loser's losses.
- **Move replay.** Pressing a line of the history that moved pieces (a move, a retreat, an advance, a move into a locale given up or taken, a feint that changed where pieces stand) opens a sheet over the table instead of leaving the present: the sentence of the action with its time, and under it the part of the map the pieces crossed, turned as the board is turned, with every piece as it stood once the action was done. The pieces that moved stand where they went; the places they left are drawn as washed-out blocks where they stood, and a corps that some units left keeps the shape it had so those places lie open beside it. A dark arrow with a light edge runs from each place left to where those pieces went, one arrow for each place they went to, bending through the locales of a road march; it starts clear of the places left and stops short of the pieces, starts back over the places left when the two are too close for an arrow between them, and for pieces coming onto the field starts at the road's entry, or farther out when the pieces stand on it. Approaches held at that moment are tinted as on the board, the moved pieces' own included, and the darker borders follow the map aid. Flags keep off the arrows and the places left. Close, Escape or a single tap outside the sheet puts it away, and it goes of itself if the action it shows is undone; *See the whole game at this point* enters History View at that action. A line that moved nothing goes straight to History View. This sheet takes the place of the shared layout's animated replay, at the player's request.
- **History View.** The action area describes the action shown and nothing on the table can be picked. The map shows the position at that step and, during an attack, the attack marker and the roles that were public then.
- **Settling.** While the session is still settling an action or an Undo, the table takes no choice: pieces are not offered for picking up or naming, and the controls of the action area and the Undo button do not respond. Nothing changes appearance for it. What was picked before stays as it is until the new position arrives.

## Coexistence and precedence

- **A choice changes only itself.** This is a deliberate departure from the shared staged-selection rule that setting a stage clears the later ones, made at the player's request and carried by the two helpers `revise` and `withdraw`. Within one step, changing a choice never resets another that does not depend on it: the units chosen, the command and the Guard Attack announcement of an order; the command, width and leading units of an attack; where retreating units go, which unit stays with a commander and which takes a loss; which units advance and which commanders go with them; the command and the Guard Attack announcement of an order. Undo still takes the choices back one at a time.
- **One Undo.** The header's Undo is the only way back; no panel has a cancel or back control. An action that showed its player an enemy block they could not see (pressing an attack against leading units, losses that fall on a hidden block, the deal of the armies after the auction, the end of the game) is not offered for Undo. Pressing the choice already in force on a switch, or the leading unit already struck, is no choice and adds no step. It steps back through the set-up edits while deploying, through the picks of the attack step in front of the player during an attack, and through the command being built otherwise, one manual choice at a time; with none left it undoes the last committed action. Choices the table makes by itself (the units a pick-up starts with, the riding-on selection, the attackers taken from the threat, the default retreat) are never a step of their own.
- Within an attack step Undo takes the latest stage first: a retreat being arranged is unwound before the choice to retreat, and that before the defenders picked earlier, which are still there when the player comes back to them.
- Picking up pieces is possible only in the command phase; an attack in progress replaces it, so the selection ring, the orders in the action area and move targets never coexist with battle picking.
- Retreat planning takes precedence over naming pieces: while a retreat is being arranged, blocks pick a unit to send by itself and locales are retreat targets.
- Riding on yields to any pick-up by the player, of another group or of another unit in the riders' own group; while nothing else is picked up it takes the place of the empty selection.
- Choosing the road replaces the marks of the places to go with the marks of the roads to one place; Undo, or changing the units or the command, brings the places back. The legend lists its marks as marches by road.
- Battle focus and whole-field picking cannot overlap: the second happens only in the command phase.
- Dimming for "left out of the command" applies only to the picked-up group; dimming for "no part in this attack" applies to every piece while an attack is on. The two never apply together.
- The army as arranged in set-up replaces the live position on the map only for the deploying player and only until the set-up is committed or the taps are undone.
- The move replay is a modal sheet over a table that stays as it is: it never changes the position shown, the selection, the view of the board or what Undo would do, and it can be opened in the present or from within History View. Only one is open at a time. New actions arriving underneath leave it open; it closes only when the action it shows is no longer in the history shown.
- History View and settling both withhold every choice; History View also hides Undo and replaces the action area's content.

| Board view | How the board is turned | Table on mount (by screen shape and side), the view control | Board, piece layout, flags, battle and selection focus | Kept for the session; never reset by game state |
| Zoom | On-screen scale of the board | The shared scaling wrapper, whose scale the session reads through the table | Flags and markers that keep a readable size, whole-field picking | Derived from the wrapper's own state; a whole-field value stands in until the table is drawn |
| Command selection | Group picked up, units left in, command kind, Guard Attack announcement, and the place a march is bound for with the taps along its road | Pieces layer, target layer, action area | Pieces layer, target layers, action area, header Undo | Staged selection holding only the player's own choices; reset when a new position arrives; applies only in the owner's command phase in Live View; a group or unit no longer in the position is ignored |
| Riding-on selection | The cavalry road move still open, shown as the picked-up group with the riding units in hand, and whether they ride on or take a new command | Session, from the game state's road move and the command selection | Pieces layer, target layers, action area | Derived; lasts while the game state holds the road move and the player has picked up nothing else, neither another group nor another unit of the riders' group; a new command is the player's own choice within it and exists only for riders that can still be commanded |
| Road choice | The place chosen and where the roads to it part next | Session, from the command selection and the places on offer | Target layer, action area, legend | Derived; exists only while the chosen place is still on offer by more than one road, so it goes with the selection, with a change of units or command, and with a new position |
| Battle selections | The acting player's picks, one staged selection per attack step | Map blocks, battle panel | Battle step, header Undo | Reset when a new position arrives; each applies only while its own step is in front of the player |
| Battle step | What the acting player is deciding: who may be tapped, what each named piece is doing, the retreat as it stands with its defaults, and whether the step can be committed | Session, from the attack (the attacker's own plan in it included) and the battle selections | Pieces layer, battle panel, target layer, attack marker layer | Derived; exists only for the acting player in Live View |
| Block marks | Whether a block can be picked, its role in the attack, and whether it is dimmed or marked as moved | Session, from the battle step, the roles already public and the command selection | Pieces layer, the battle panel's summary of forces, the orders' unit strip | Derived; in History View and for the other player only public roles and the dimming remain, and no moved mark |
| Commander flags | Where each flag stands, which way it flies, what it tallies and whether its commander has commanded | Session, from the piece layout, the attack marker, the board view and the zoom | Flag layer | Derived; the side a flag flies can change with the zoom, because flags keep a readable size |
| Held approaches | Which approaches have pieces blocking them, and whose | Session, from the piece layout | Held-approach layer | Derived; follows the position shown, including the army as arranged in set-up |
| Darker borders | Whether the border tint is shown | The player's title preference, changed by its switch | Border-tint layer, the switch | Saved to the account; shown at once and reverted if the save fails; never reset by game state |
| Approach symbols | Whether the approach symbols are shown | Its switch | Approach-symbol layer, the switch | Kept for the session; never reset by game state |
| Command discs | Which boxes of the printed command tracks are spent this turn | Session, from the commanders and the armies' command counts | Command-disc layer | Derived; follows the position shown |

## Render ownership

- **Dimming, picking, roles and moved marks of a block** come from the block marks alone. The pieces layer is their only renderer on the map. The battle panel's summary of forces shows the session's roles (the step's picks over the roles already public, a retreat being arranged first), and the orders' unit strip takes its moved dimming from the same rule; neither works out a role or a dimming of its own.
- **Dimming is a change of paint, never of opacity.** A dimmed block, on the map or on a tile, is drawn solid in a washed-out tone of its army's colour with its symbols toned to match, so the map never shows through a piece and the two armies stay distinguishable.
- **Places to go** are one list. The target layer draws it twice around the pieces: locales and approaches to block underneath, so a block always takes the tap where the two overlap, and threat arrows on top, because they sit on approaches where blocks stand. A threat arrow has the attack marker's shape and size, in ink where the marker is red, and turns red under the pointer.
- **Arrows are never covered.** Threat arrows, the attack marker and retreat arrows are the top layers of the board, over the flags; the attack marker and retreat arrows take no taps. A threat arrow hands the session the board point it was tapped at, and the session decides whether a block of the chosen group lies under it; the layer does no hit-testing of its own.
- **Selection ring** belongs to the pieces layer, over the blocks. **Commander flags** are drawn over the pieces and the approach symbols but under every arrow, and keep clear of the arrows where they can. Neither takes taps; a group is picked up by its blocks. Where a flag stands and which way it flies is decided once, in the session, from every block and flag on the board; the flag component only draws it.
- **Held-approach tints and track markers** (the command discs and the morale and time markers, drawn by one layer as the same disc) are the lowest layers on the board art, under the places to go and the pieces, and take no taps. The markers' positions are measured from the printed tracks, so they must be measured again if the board art changes.
- **Set-up locales** are a layer of their own, above the held-approach tints and under the places to go, so a marked locale or approach takes the tap where they overlap. They tint the set-up locale of each corps that starts on the map and take the tap that opens it. The flag of a corps with no unit yet is planted by the flag layer at that locale's stack point, and is placed with the other flags so none covers another.
- **Locale shapes**, which mark the places to go and take their taps, are built from the printed bars of the public map file: each side lies on the centre line of its bar, and where the map prints no bar the shape ends at the edge of the battlefield. The bars meeting at a junction do not all pass through one point, so each shape stops where its sides reach the small patch in which those lines cross one another; that patch belongs to no locale, and neighbouring shapes do not overlap. Reserve stacks stand at the roomiest point of their locale.
- **Approach symbols** are drawn over the pieces and under the attack marker and flags, and take no taps. They and the border tint come from the bars and marks of the public map file, not from the rules data, so they show what is printed.
- **The colours of the places to go** are named values set once on the table and used by both the target layer and the legend, so the legend always shows the map's own marks.
- **The attack arrow's shape** is defined once and used both to draw the marker and to keep flags off it.
- **The move replay** draws its own small map from the same parts as the board (the piece layout, the block, the contact shadow, the flag placement, the held-approach and border-tint layers), so a piece looks the same in both. Where the pieces stood before and after the action is read from the recorded history with the shared recorded-history reader, which never moves the history cursor and never touches the game. The session works the scene out from those two positions and the action's own record in one place (who stands where, the places left, each arrow with its shaft and head, the points flags must keep off, the frame); the sheet measures only its own width, to size the flags, and draws. An arrow's shaft ends inside its head, both are edged before either is filled, and a line too short for a full head gets a smaller arrow rather than a head with no shaft. Nothing in the sheet takes a tap except its two controls.
- **Empty map** puts pieces down. Blocks and targets keep their own taps from reaching it.
- **Board turning** is one transform on the board. The piece layout decides every block's angle and drawing order: reserve stacks are laid out for the turned board so they stay readable, blocking pieces by their approach alone. Flags, count badges and retreat counts turn back to stay upright.
- **The board is drawn at the scale it is shown.** The shared scaling wrapper lays the board out again at the view's scale once the view rests and stretches it only while the view is moving, so the board costs what is on screen. Marks that follow the zoom read the wrapper's scale; nothing measures the board on screen.
- **The contact shadow** is cast by each stack and each track marker on its own, on the board and in the move replay. A shadow over a whole layer of pieces would blur an area the size of the board on every repaint.
- **Moving the view** belongs to the table alone, through the shared scaling wrapper: battle focus, its return, the set-up view, whole-field picking, finding a corps, the command-track shortcut, and bringing picked-up pieces out from under the orders sheet. The map menu only asks for the command track and for the board to be turned. Nothing watches state to move the view: the table hears of an attack beginning or ending from the session's state-change listener, which is how it tells a played action from Undo or a history step, and it is told by the session when pieces are picked up. It measures the orders sheet itself at that moment. No layer pans or zooms.
- **The action area** owns every prompt and control, the orders for chosen pieces included. With nothing chosen the command phase is one sentence, "Choose a corps or unit on the map or [End turn]", naming any corps that can be brought on. With pieces chosen their name takes that line and a sheet hangs from the band over the top of the board: the unit strip, then the commands on offer with End turn at the right, then guidance. The band itself keeps its height, so choosing never resizes the board, and the scaling wrapper keeps focused views clear of the sheet by its measured height; the table moves the board only when the pieces just picked up would lie under the sheet. The command switch sits under a small "Command" label, as in an attack. Nothing else but the map menu lies over the board.
- **Colours of the action area** are a small set of named values (ground, ink, warning, hover) set once on the table and used by every panel, button and unit tile inside it, so the area can change ground without any panel knowing.
- **Acting for a player needs their army in view.** The session offers no choices, set-up included, while the state on hand hides the faces of the acting player's own units. That happens for a moment when an administrator switches Admin mode on in a game they are not seated in as that player: the acting player is named at once and the full state follows.
- **Withholding choices while settling** is decided in the session for the map and, for the controls of the masthead and of the action area, by a disabled group around each that follows the same state. Styles that show a control as unavailable follow the control's own state, so a settling table does not flicker.
