# Napoleon's Triumph UI interaction visual contract

## Visual intents

- **Board view.** The board is drawn north up, or turned a quarter so one army's side of the table is at the bottom of the screen. A wide screen opens on the viewer's own lines; a tall one opens north up. The switch at the top of the map menu chooses among the three views. Stacks in reserve are laid out to read upright in whichever view is shown, and a player's own stacks are put in reading order (see _Order within a stack_); commander flags and count badges stay upright.
- **Standing clear.** Pieces in reserve gather on their locale's anchor. When pieces already placed lie in the way there, most often a corps blocking one of the locale's own approaches, the whole reserve stands aside to the clearest place inside its locale, as near the anchor as it can. Blocking pieces keep to their approach, but on a wide approach they take whichever half lies across less of what is already placed. Where a locale has no clear room, stacks may still overlap. A reserve keeps the same clear gap from the pieces fronting an approach as groups in one file keep from each other, wherever its locale has the room, so the two are not read as one stack.
- **Fronting an approach.** Pieces blocking an approach line up along it, inside their locale, and face across it. Each group (a corps, or the detached units there) stands in single file; a wide approach holds two files, so two groups front it side by side and a lone group leaves half of it open, while further groups fall in behind the shorter file. Groups in one file stand a clear gap apart, wider than the gap between the blocks of a group and kept even where a deep file closes its own blocks up over one another, so a detached unit behind a corps is never read as part of the corps; a file with a gap in it closes its blocks up a little more to make the room. In each file the top of each block's face points at the approach, and the block at the approach lies whole on top with the ranks behind it tucked under. This holds in every board view, so a block facing down the screen shows its face upside down.
- **Order within a stack.** A player's own pieces stand in a fixed order in every group, so a stack reads the same way each time: Guard, then infantry, then cavalry, then artillery, and the strongest first within each. A reserve stack runs in that order from its top down. A file fronting an approach runs in that order from the top of the screen down where it stands more upright than level on screen, and from inside its locale out to the approach where it lies level; the block at the approach still lies whole on top. The unit strip of the orders and the viewer's own corps in the armies panel follow the same order. The other army's stacks, and anyone's when their faces are not all known to the viewer, are left as the game holds them, so their order tells nothing. The one exception keeps it that way: the units such a stack has committed to the attack in progress are shown at the end of it nearest their approach (see _Whose order is whose in an attack_).
- **Stacks closed up and spread out.** Where a locale is short of room its stacks close up, each block lying over part of the next. A closed-up stack is drawn like a rank fronting an approach: one block lies whole on top (the top one of a reserve stack, the front one at an approach) and each block under it shows its lower edge, so the blocks can always be counted. The blocks underneath keep their symbols, so whatever part of a face is not covered still shows; the whole of it is read by spreading the stack. Tapping a closed-up stack spreads every stack of its locale to a full block apart: a stack the viewer may pick up spreads as it is picked up and closes when it is put down; any other stack (the other army's, or anyone's outside the viewer's command phase and in History View) spreads at the tap and closes at a second tap on it, a tap on the empty map, or when a new position arrives. Only one locale is spread this way at a time, besides the locales of an attack, which are always spread. Spread stacks may lie over their locale's approach bars and over neighbouring stacks.
- **Held approaches.** The bar of an approach with pieces blocking it is tinted in their army's colour on their locale's side, for both players, in History View and for the army as arranged in set-up, so two armies facing each other across one approach each colour their own half.
- **Commander flags.** A corps's flag stands on the top of its stack as the board is turned, so the banner clears its own blocks at any angle: at the highest point by choice, at the other end of the top block when that covers less. It flies to the right unless flying left covers less. What it avoids, in order of weight, is the arrows (the attack marker and any threat arrows on offer), then other stacks and flags already placed. The banner is always opaque. It carries the commander's name and the number of units in the corps, the same for both armies; the kinds of unit are read from the blocks and the armies panel, never from the flag.
- **Map menu.** The board's own controls fold behind one _Map_ button, marked with three lines, at the top right corner of the part of the map that can be seen: under the header, and under the orders sheet while it hangs over the board, beside the legend where there is room and below it where the legend runs across. The button opens and closes the menu on every screen, and the menu stays as it was left until the button is pressed again. Open, it hangs below the button, the board view first: a three-way sliding switch, _Allies_, _French_ and _North_ with a thick up arrow, its thumb on the view shown. Under it come _Save image_ with its _with unit types_ switch beside it, then the three map aids. Everything in the menu is a light paper chip like the map legend, never dark: the aids show whether they are on by a small ring that fills, and the board view's thumb is a tint of the paper with the name on it still in ink.
- **Saving a picture.** _Save image_ in the map menu makes a JPEG of the whole board, turned as the board is turned at that moment, at the board art's own size, and hands it to the browser as a download named for the game and the round shown. It holds the printed board (with the printed set-up marks while the armies set up), the track markers, the held-approach tints, the pieces and the commander flags, exactly as they stand on the viewer's screen: their own faces, the other army's blocks as they see them, flags at the size the current zoom gives them. It leaves out everything temporary or chosen by the viewer: places to go, road-choice marks, the selection ring, the attack marker, closed-reserve lines, the map aids, the legend and the menu. A switch beside the button, _with unit types_, on to begin with and kept for the session, decides whether the picture tells what the units are: off, every block in it is blank, the viewer's own included, each flag gives only its count of units, and the file's name ends _unit-types-hidden_, so the picture can be passed on without giving an army away. The board on screen is not changed by this, not even for a frame. The button reads _Saving…_ and takes no press while the picture is made.
- **Map aids.** Three switches in the map menu, all off to begin with. _Darker borders_ is the player's own preference, saved to their account and the same in every game of the title; _Approach symbols_ and _Moves this turn_ are kept for the session. _Darker borders_ lays a light tint over every printed approach bar, impassable ones included, so locales are easier to tell apart; it stops at the edges of the title, the order-of-battle strips and the tracks. _Approach symbols_ draws each side's printed marks (infantry, cavalry and artillery penalties, obstruction, impassable) beside its bar, in the printed order and proportions but larger and over the pieces, for when pieces cover the printed ones.
- **Moves this turn.** With the switch on, every move made since the last turn ended is drawn on the board as an arrow, shaped like the arrows of the history pictures with the same light edge, but in the colour of the army whose pieces moved, so the two armies' moves are told apart at a glance, and a little see-through, so the map shows faintly under them while their numbers stay solid; the history pictures, which show one action, keep their dark arrow: from where the pieces stood to just short of where they stand now, bending along the road they marched by and starting at the map edge for pieces that came on. Both armies' moves are drawn, so the trails of a turn include the retreats it forced. Beside the middle of each arrow stands the number of its move in the order the moves were made, counting from one; the arrows of one action, such as a retreat to two locales, carry the same number. The numbers are plain numerals in the arrow's own colour with its light edge, with no disc or badge behind them; they stand on the upper side of the arrow as the board is turned (to the right of one running straight up or down), read upright in every board view, and grow as the flags do when the board is seen from far off. Pieces that moved more than once in the turn have an arrow for each move, the second starting where the first ends. In a stack the viewer does not read, an arrow starts from the stack as a whole and ends at the stack as a whole, so it does not point at which of the stack moved; in the viewer's own it runs from and to the very blocks. An arrow starts clear of any piece still standing where the movers left, and one whose pieces have moved on or are gone from the board ends where they stopped. The arrows are drawn over the pieces and under the approach symbols, the flags and the attack marker, take no taps, and the commander flags step aside for them and their numbers as they do for the attack marker. They follow the board as it is shown: Undo takes a move's arrow away with the move, a turn ending clears them, and in History View they are the moves of the turn shown. They are no part of a saved picture.
- **Command tracks.** A black disc lies on a commander's box of the printed command track once that commander has given a command this turn, and on one Independent box for each independent command used. Both armies' tracks show for both players and in History View; the discs go when the turn ends. The tracks are printed along one edge of the board, so a turned board resting at its covering fit can leave the viewer's own track out of sight; the map is panned to it by hand.
- **Morale and time tracks.** Each army's morale is a disc in its own colour on the printed Morale Track, and the round is a black disc on the printed Time Track. A disc lies in the half of its box away from the printed number or hour, so the print stays readable under it; two armies at one level lie side by side in that half. Before play begins the time disc is on the scenario's start box, and when the game is over it stays on the last round played; an army has no morale disc before the armies are chosen or once it has broken. Both show for both players and in History View.
- **Choosing pieces.** On their turn a player taps a corps or a group of detached units on the map. The group is ringed, its orders open in the action area, and every place it can go is marked on the map: locales to move into (road marches in a second tint), approaches to block, and arrows for approaches it can threaten. A corps is chosen whole: its units sit in the unit strip inside one outline, none marked on its own, and the command is a Corps Move. Tapping units, in the strip or on the corps's own blocks on the map, chooses the ones to send instead; both drive the same choice: each is outlined, the blocks left out are dimmed on the map, and the command switches to a Unit Move for one unit or a Detach Move for several, with the Corps Move still on offer. Choosing every unit, or none, is the whole corps again. In a group of detached units one unit is chosen at a time: the block tapped is the one picked up, tapping another switches to it, and tapping the chosen one puts it down. The exception is two artillery units blocking a wide approach, which may attack across it together, each by its own Unit Move: tapping the second adds it to the first, the switch stays on Unit Move, a line says the two fire together, and the only place marked is the threat across their approach. Two artillery units chosen out of a corps on a wide approach are offered the same Unit Move beside the Detach Move, when the corps keeps another unit. Announcing a Guard Attack is offered only when the chosen units include Guard infantry and can threaten across an approach that is not obstructed; once announced, only those threats stay marked. Tapping empty map puts the pieces down. A threat arrow lies over the pieces that can make the threat, so a tap that falls on one of the chosen group's own blocks under an arrow chooses the unit, and the arrow takes the tap everywhere else. When the pieces picked up would lie under the orders sheet, the board is moved, at the zoom it has, to bring them out from under it.
- **Army colours.** The French are always blue and the Allies always red, as their pieces are printed. The title does not take the colours a player prefers on the site, so a seat is never drawn in another colour.
- **Units the enemy has seen.** A player sees every one of their own units face-up, so those the enemy has also seen are marked: a small open eye at the left end of the block, in the block's own ink, on the map, in the armies panel, in the orders and in the panels of an attack, and in the history pictures as things stood then. A unit is seen from the moment it is turned face-up to the enemy (leading, taking a loss, riding by road, retreating, the fixed battery always) until the shuffle at the end of the turn hides it again (rule 7). Only the owner sees the mark; to the enemy a seen unit is simply the one whose face shows. It is left off a picture saved with the unit types hidden.
- **The fixed battery.** The battery that never moves is marked for both players wherever its block is drawn: two small triangles in the block's ink, one set in from each end and pointing inward at the unit's symbol. It is always face-up, so it carries no eye. A role's letter or a loss printed on it stands just inside the right-hand triangle, beside its one symbol, and not where it stands on other blocks. The mark is left off, with the face, in a picture saved without unit types.
- **Units already moved.** While no attack is on, a player sees a dot at the end of each of their own blocks that has moved this turn; in their command phase the lettering of a commander's flag is muted once the commander has given a command.
- **Commands left.** In the command phase the action area always says what the army may still command, in the rule's own terms: for the Allies the corps commands left of their five, for the French that there is no limit beyond one per corps, and for both the independent commands left. The armies panel gives the commands used, with the same limits. It is always the last line of the instructions, on a line of its own under whatever guidance the panel is giving.
- **Command switch.** Under the unit strip, and in an attack under the attacker's pieces, one three-way switch names the command by its rulebook name: Corps Move, Detach Move, Unit Move, always in that order. In an attack it sits under a small "Command" label, and across a wide approach the width is a second switch of the same kind, Wide or Narrow, under an "Attack width" label. The command in force is filled, and the thumb slides to it when it changes; commands the chosen units cannot be given are greyed and do not respond. It is never plain text, so a whole corps shows Corps Move on the switch with the other two greyed until units are tapped.
- **Map legend.** While pieces are chosen, a small key lies on the top edge of the map, just under the action area, naming the marks now on the map: move, march by road, choose which way, block an approach, threaten an attack, and closed to other units by a road march. Each entry shows the mark as it is drawn on the board, on a patch of the map's tone, and only marks actually on the map are listed, so an arrow in the key means there is an arrow to tap. The key is a light paper chip with rounded corners and its content centred in even padding, never dark and never the page's colour; it takes no taps, and lets taps through to the map beneath it. Threat arrows also carry a tooltip.
- **Armies panel.** Each corps lists its units as blocks in a tight grid, three to a row, with no tile chrome; they are for reading, not for tapping.
- **Finding a corps.** In the armies panel the location of every corps on the map, of either army, is a button with a target mark. Pressing it centres the board on that corps's locale and its neighbours; on a phone the table also scrolls from the panel to the board. It changes the view only, never the selection, and counts as moving the view by hand.
- **Riding on.** Cavalry that has taken a locale by road and may still ride on stays picked up without being asked: the action area says it is riding on, the riding units cannot be left out of the ride, and the map marks only what the same command still allows, which is locales and approaches along its road and arrows for the approaches it could threaten across. Picking up other pieces and commanding them ends the ride. The ride belongs to the units that ride, not to the group they stand in: when the cavalry has ridden into a locale where other detached units stand, those are in its group and its unit strip, left out of the ride, and tapping one that can still be commanded chooses it for a command of its own, as in any group of detached units. Tapping the cavalry, or the chosen unit again, takes the ride up again. The cavalry is not marked as moved in the strip while its ride is open. Cavalry that can still take a command of its own, which is French reinforcement cavalry in its turn of entry, gets a two-way switch beside the command switch, _Riding on_ and _New command_, in the same sliding style. _Riding on_ is in force unasked; _New command_ offers the commands and marks the places of an ordinary pick-up, a line says that it ends the ride and is the second command of the turn of entry, and Undo returns to the ride. Riders in a corps can then be chosen among like the units of any corps. The same switch stands above the command switch in an attack wherever the riders named could attack either way, starting from what the player had chosen when threatening: at the feint and when following up a retreat. In the declaration of a pressed attack there is no switch, because an attack by road must be a feint: the riders are listed with the pieces in reserve and attack by a new command. Once the cavalry has used its road allowance, or a corps has had to halt beside an enemy corps, it stays picked up only to end blocking an approach of its locale that its road crosses; no locale and no threat is marked then.
- **Choosing the road.** A place that can be reached by more than one road is still marked once. When the roads leave different things behind for the rest of the turn (rule 10: a corps of two or more units closes the reserves it marches into to other units, and a reinforcement goes on only by roads joined to the one it came on by), tapping the place does not move the pieces yet: the place is outlined as chosen, the other marks go, and the map marks where the roads part, which is the locales to march through or, for roads that come on in one locale, the points on the map edge where they enter. These marks are a choice between roads, not places to go, and look it: a solid outline and a stronger tint in a colour used for nothing else on the map, orange when the Allies march and violet when the French do. Each carries a thick curved arrow in that colour's line tone with a paper edge and no disc behind it, showing the way through: it comes in from where the march comes (the pieces themselves at the first parting, the locale before it afterwards) and curves towards the locale the road goes on to, drawn as large as its sign allows and centred on the locale's stack point. A road that comes on at the map edge has a straight arrow standing on its entry mark and pointing straight onto the board. The arrow keeps the board's directions as the board is turned and keeps a readable size at any zoom. It lies over the pieces and takes the tap too, so a parting covered by stacks can still be chosen; it does not change under the pointer, and the pointer resting on it counts as resting on its locale, whose highlight stays as it is. A line in the action area says why. A tap on one of those marks makes the move; where the roads still part after it, the next parting is marked, and Undo takes the taps back one at a time before it takes back the place. Roads that leave the same behind are one road, the shortest standing for them, so a single unit already on the map is never asked. A place that can be gone to on foot is offered on foot only.
- **Reserves closed by a road march.** A reserve that a corps of two or more units has marched into is closed to other units for the rest of the turn (rule 10). Such a locale carries a very faint wash with thin diagonal lines set well apart, for both players, in History View too, until the turn ends. It is an overlay, not a mark to tap: it lies under the places to go and the pieces, takes no taps, and has no outline or sign. While pieces are chosen the legend names it: _closed to other units by a road march_. The lines rise to the right on screen whichever way the board is turned, as they do in the legend, and keep a readable weight and spacing at any zoom.
- **Reinforcements.** Corps still off the map are picked up from the action area; their entry locales and road marches are then marked like any other move. Units a corps left off the map when it came on are offered beside them as _Units left behind_ and come on one at a time, each by a Unit Move.
- **Battle focus.** While an attack is being fought the view closes in on the two locales and returns to where it was when the attack is over. The view glides there when an action just played begins or ends the attack; on Undo, when stepping through the history, and when a position is restored it cuts, with no motion. Panning or zooming by hand cancels the return. There is somewhere to return to only if the attack began while the table was open and the board has not been turned since: when the table opens on an attack already under way, or the board is turned during one, the attack ends on the whole board instead, cut to with no motion. Pieces in the two locales spread to full spacing so each block can be tapped. Every piece that has no part in the attack so far and cannot be picked for it is dimmed.
- **Pieces that cannot be picked.** In a step where some of the pieces shown may not be picked under the rules (foot in a counter-attack while the defence is holding, artillery always), they stay in the panel but disabled, painted in the washed-out tone, and cannot be tapped on the map. Tiles that are only there to be read (the forces named so far) are painted at full strength.
- **Naming pieces in an attack.** While naming the defending pieces, at the feint decision, while naming the defence's leading units, at the attack declaration, the counter-attack, the loss assignment and the forced advance, the acting player taps blocks on the map or the matching tiles in the panel; both drive the same pick. A named block carries a letter at its end and stays in its stack: D for a defender, A for an attacker, C for a counter-attacker, and L for a leading unit of either side, since whose block it is already says which. The letter turns with the block. A unit picked in a step with no letter of its own (the advance, a retreat by itself) carries a plain lozenge. The panel's tiles carry the same letter on the piece and spell the role out in a word underneath. The moved dots are hidden during an attack, so the two never share a block. When the defence stands, the attacker is asked one thing: press the attack or call it a feint. No pieces or command are offered then, because a pressed attack names them only after the defender has named the defence's leading units (rule 11, steps 4 and 5). Calling it a feint opens the naming of the feinting pieces and where they end, and Undo goes back to the question; when the attack cannot be pressed (an attack by road against a defence that stands) the naming opens at once. At the declaration the attacker's pick starts from the command and pieces that made the threat; once the attacker names other pieces, the command is the first one those pieces can take, not the one the threat was planned with. The game keeps those for the attacker alone, so the start is the same after a reload or on another device, and the prompt says so only when it is so. A forced advance with a single unit and nothing to choose is made by the game and never asked; with a single unit whose commander may stay or go, the unit is already named and only the commander is asked about. A road attack names its command like any other: one cavalry unit of a larger corps goes by Unit Move, with the Corps Move on the switch for taking its commander along. Two batteries on a wide approach combine by naming both to lead. When a narrow attack meets two leading units, the attacker taps the one it strikes, on the map or on its tile; the other loses its L, being no longer a leading unit (rule 11, step 5).
- **Whose order is whose in an attack.** Where a named unit stands among the others of its stack is its owner's to know. The owner sees the letters on their own blocks where those blocks stand in the owner's reading order. Everyone else sees the same stack with its named units brought to the end nearest the approach they fight at, whichever of the stack they really are: leading units outermost, then counter-attackers, then the other attackers or defenders, and the rest of the stack behind them as the game holds it. In a stack blocking the approach that end is the bar; in a reserve stack it is the top when the approach is higher on the screen than the locale's anchor and the bottom when it is lower. When the attack is over the stack goes back to how the game holds it. The history pictures follow the same rule and carry it further, since a picture washes blocks out and so could point at one: in a stack the viewer does not read, the places units left stand at the end of the stack towards where they went, and units lost and any other units the action names stand at the end towards the attack's approach, never where those units really were. The owner's own pictures keep every block in its place.
- **What a step lists.** The action area lists only the units a step is about: the ones that can be named in it and the ones already named. The defender's other units in the locale, which cannot defend this approach, are not listed in the defence step; they are on the map, and the retreat step lists them if ground is given.
- **The defence in two steps.** The defender is asked twice, as the rules ask (rule 11, steps 2 and 4), and never for both things at once. First, against the threat: which pieces defend, or to retreat; a tap names a unit a defender and a second tap takes it back, and the prompt says nothing of who leads, now or later. Pieces blocking the approach all defend and cannot retreat, so then the defender is not asked at all and the attacker is asked at once to press or feint. (An attack that was already waiting on such a defender when the game began naming them itself still shows the defender the pieces, named, with only _Defend_ to press.) (Likewise an attack whose defender had already named leading units together with the pieces: its attacker is told that pressing shows those units, since it still does.) Second, only if the attack is pressed: which of the defending pieces lead, one on a narrow approach and up to two on a wide one, or none; a tap makes a defender a leading unit and a second tap makes it a plain defender again, and the button reads _Lead with this unit_, _Lead with these units_ or _Defend with no leading unit_. The prompt says the leading units are shown to the enemy as they are named. Pressing the attack therefore shows the attacker nothing, and what is revealed is revealed by the defender's own choice. A Guard Attack, which cannot be called off, goes from the first question straight to the second. While the defender names the leading units the attacker sees _Waiting for … to name the leading units of the defence_.
- **Results of an attack.** The initial and the final result of an attack are each set in the middle of a rule like the one under the masthead, tapering away to either side, the words in italic and the number upright and heavier: _Initial result +2_. The rule is as wide as the prompt, not the whole panel, so the number stays over the controls it belongs to. While the attacker declares, the rule stands above the _Attack_ button and shows the initial result the pieces named so far would give; while the defender decides on a counter-attack it stands above that button with the final result the choice would give. Neither is shown while the step has a problem to report, which takes the place beside the button. Once a result is settled its rule moves up under the two forces, and when both are settled they share one rule, a lozenge between them. An attack called off as a feint has no result, so once the feint is declared the rule under the forces carries the one word _Feint_ instead. The panel reads in the order the attack went: the attacking and defending forces side by side, each unit's part told by the letter on its block alone with no word under it, the units standing close together since none of them can be picked, then any units that counter-attacked in a row of their own headed _Counter-attacking_, then the rule of the results. A unit that counter-attacked is listed in both, as the defender or leading unit it was and as a counter-attacker.
- **Accounting for a result.** A result in a rule is a button, marked by a dotted underline: pressing it opens a small sheet in the middle of the screen, over a dimmed table, that shows how the number is reached. The sheet names the result and its value, says in a sentence what the result is, and then lists what was counted, one line each with what it added or took away: the units leading the attack, the step infantry lose against a blocked approach, the approach's penalty, the units leading the defence, what a Guard Attack gives back, that artillery leading sets the defence's leading units aside, and for the final result the initial result and the counter-attack. Lines that count units show those units as blocks in their army's colour, as they were when they were counted and not as losses have left them since. The lines end in a ruled total that is the result. The sheet of a final result adds one sentence saying which way it decides the attack and, at zero, what broke the tie. The sheet works the same for a result still being decided, from the pieces named so far, and for one the game has settled. It closes on _Close_, on Escape and on a press outside it, and changes nothing. A result settled before the game kept its account, in an attack already under way when this was introduced, is plain words and opens nothing.
- **Losses of an attack.** Once an attack has cost either army anything, a second rule says what: each army by name with the steps it has lost and the morale it has paid so far, or _no loss_, the attacker first, a lozenge between them. It counts everything since the attack was threatened, a retreat's losses included, and it grows as losses are assigned. Under the rule of the losses stand the units that have lost steps, the attacker's and then the defender's, each as the block it was before with what it lost printed on it and _destroyed_ under it when it was; they are at the size of every other unit in the panel, begin at the left like every other row, stand close together, and are followed by a little room before the step's own prompt. Between the rule of the results and the rule of the losses stands one sentence, in the size of a prompt with room above and below it, saying which way the settled result decides the attack (_the attack succeeds_ or _the attack is defeated_) and, at zero, what broke the tie; it is there from the moment the final result is settled, losses or none, so the two rules never stand one on the other once the fight is decided. The prompts of the steps that follow do not repeat it. The whole rule of the losses is a button with the same dotted underline as a result, and opens a sheet like the one that accounts for a result. That sheet gives only the figures and the reason for each, never the units, which the panel shows; it has a part for each army: the steps the result made it owe, line by line as the rules count them, once the result is settled and the game kept its account; and its morale, which is the steps it lost if it lost the fight or retreated, nothing for its steps if it won, and whatever it paid for committing the Guard or heavy cavalry or for a defeated Guard Attack.
- **The sheets that account for a number** share one form: the title with _Close_ beside it, a rule, one or two sentences saying what the number is, a second rule, and then the lines.
- **Stepping up to the approach.** When defending units step up to block the approach, every one of them is picked to begin with, and the player taps a unit to hold it back; at least one must go. Each commander among them is drawn at the end of the row as his flag, the same small banner on a staff the retreat uses, with a word under it as under a unit: _stays_ while the flag is not picked, when the units that step up are detached and he stays behind, and _advances_ once it is tapped, when he goes with them. When every unit standing with him advances he must go, so his flag is shown picked and does not answer a tap. The prompt above follows the flag: with it not picked it says the units going are detached from their corps; with it picked it names the commander as joining them and says the units left behind detach and remain in reserve, when there are any. The _Advance to the approach_ button stands on a line of its own under the row, as the button of every other step of an attack does.
- **Regrouping.** When a defeated attack breaks a corps up, the step lists the corps's units in a row with the commander's flag standing after the unit he keeps; tapping another unit of the corps moves the flag to it. There is one row for each corps, so the flag is never picked up first as it is in a retreat, where a tap on a unit means something else.
- **Retreat.** A retreat is arranged in up to two steps, the losses first, because a unit that loses its last step does not retreat and so changes who needs a place. A rule like the rule of the results, _The retreat costs 2 steps_, heads both steps whenever the retreat costs anything; it is a button with the dotted underline of the other numbers and opens a sheet of the same form as theirs, _Retreat losses_, which gives the reason for each group that owes steps (the infantry in reserve and the width of the approach attacked, or the approach a group blocks and its width), the total, that every unit that retreats is shown to the enemy, and who loses nothing. _The losses._ This step is asked only when the steps of some group can fall on its units in more than one way. It lists the units that owe steps, a small faint line _Owes 2 steps_ over each group when there is more than one group, and the steps each unit is to lose are printed on its tile. The number of steps is fixed, so the panel never lets it be raised or lowered: the losses start spread as the table suggests, and tapping a unit moves one step of its group's loss onto it from the unit of the group carrying most. A unit that owes its loss alone, or that can take no more, does not respond. _Take these losses_ goes on to the second step. No locale is offered on the map meanwhile. _Where they go._ The units are listed in rows by where they are going, each row named before its units (_To_ and the locale), then a row _No room: lost_ for units that no locale has room for, then a row _Lost_ for the artillery, which cannot retreat, and for units their losses destroy; the units of those two rows are dimmed and the last row says which it is under each tile. On a narrow screen a row's name stands on a line above its units. Locales the retreat can reach are marked on the map and an arrow with a count shows where the units are going. To begin with every unit goes to the one locale with most room, and another locale is used only when that one is full; the table never parts a retreat of its own accord. Tapping a locale sends every unit there; no locale is offered while a flag is held. Tapping a unit, in the panel or on the map, picks it up, and the next locale tapped takes only that unit; that is offered only when more than one unit retreats and there is more than one locale to go to, or when room is short. When there is not room for every unit the line above the rows says how many will be lost, and a unit with no place that is picked up and sent takes the place of one sent earlier, the latest choice always having first claim (rule 12: the choice is the retreating player's); with only one locale to go to it is given its place at the tap, and the line says so; with no room anywhere the line says only how many are lost. A retreat breaks a corps up: its commander keeps one unit and the others retreat detached. His flag, a small banner on a staff with a stripe of the army's colour and his name, stands after the unit he keeps and goes where it goes. Where more than one of his units retreats the flag is a button: tapping it picks it up, and the next unit of that corps tapped is the one he keeps. One faint line above the rows says what a tap does now, and nothing when there is nothing to choose. The steps a unit is to lose are printed on its tile, towards the end where a role's letter goes (−1, −2), a little larger than a letter, thickened so the figure reads as heavy, and standing in the same place on every block, clear of both the end of the block and the symbols of a three-strength unit. The same holds wherever the panel shows a unit's loss. No defence roles are shown.
- **Attack marker.** The approach under attack carries an arrow from the attack locale into the defence locale for as long as the attack lasts, for both players and in History View. The arrow's colour says how far the attack has gone. While it is only threatened the arrow is ink black, nobody's colour, and it stays so while the defence stands and the attacker has yet to answer. From the moment the attacker presses the attack, or calls it off as a feint, it takes the attacking army's colour, and a Guard Attack, which cannot be called off, has it from the moment it is announced; the history's picture of pressing the attack shows it so. Once units have counter-attacked, a narrower arrow in the defending army's colour stands beside it pointing back the way the attack came, the two side by side about the middle of the bar. Every arrow keeps the same light edge. The history pictures draw the arrow as it stood with the action they show.
- **Set-up.** Every corps starts empty. The action area holds, top to bottom: one line saying what a tap does now; the units still to assign, one stack per kind of unit with its count; a row of seats, one per corps, set off by a slightly darker ground and no outline, in the order the corps stand on the board from left to right as seen from their own army's lines (the French from their left in the north, the Allies from their left in the south), whichever way the board is turned, with the corps that set up off the map after them; and the Deploy button with what is still to do beside it. One seat is open at a time, shown by a darker ground, and a unit tapped in the pool goes to the open seat. A tap anywhere on a seat opens it: its name, its slots or its ground. The exceptions are the controls inside it: tapping a unit in any seat sends that unit back to the pool, and the on-map switch of an advance-guard corps does only its own job; neither opens the seat. A seat shows a slot, tinted in the army's own colour, for each unit the corps still needs for its minimum. Once the minimum is met it shows one faint free slot, and another each time that one is filled, for as long as the corps has room: eight to a commander, less where its set-up locale holds less or units detached there take the room. A corps with no room shows no free slot, and while it is open the line at the top says which limit it has met: the eight units of a corps, what its locale holds, or the eight counting the units detached from it. A reinforcement corps (Bernadotte, Davout) keeps one needed slot, marked _not 2-str. infantry_, until it has a unit that is not two-strength infantry, and the line says so while it is open; with seven two-strength infantry and no other unit it is offered no eighth. _Detached_ shows one free slot at a time the same way. Seats are only as tall as the fullest of them needs, so the map moves down a line when a corps outgrows the rest. A pool stack that the open seat cannot take is painted in the washed-out tone and cannot be tapped; a stack with no unit left is painted the same way with its count in a muted ink. On the map each corps that starts there has its set-up locale tinted in the army's colour, and tapping the locale or the corps opens it. The open seat is always marked on the map: an open corps has its locale tinted more strongly and outlined with the dashed line of the selection ring, and its pieces ringed; the fixed battery is ringed while its seat is open; with _Detached_ open every detached unit is ringed, or only the one in hand while one is. A corps held off the map has nothing to mark. Before it has units a corps plants its flag in the locale, showing 0. The map shows the army as arranged so far, and nothing for units still in the pool. Where the open corps stands is chosen on the map: the approaches of its own locale are marked, and its reserve when it stands on an approach. The French have two more seats. _Detached_ takes up to six units set up on their own: a unit tapped in the pool is taken in hand, the locales it may stand in are marked, and tapping one stands it there in reserve; it stays in hand, ringed on the map, with the approaches of that locale marked, so a second tap puts it on an approach. _Fixed battery_ takes one artillery unit the same way, locale and then approach, and has to end on an approach; in the set-up locale of a corps it uses none of the six detachments. With the Santon rule in use the set-up starts with a battery already on the Santon, in reserve, as one of the six; it cannot be turned or taken back, and its seat only shows it. A unit standing on its own is taken in hand again by tapping it on the map. Which commander a detached unit is counted as coming from is never asked and never shown: a detached unit appears only in _Detached_ (or _Fixed battery_), and every corps seat asks for its whole minimum for as long as the pool can fill every corps. Only when the pool cannot are detached units counted towards the corps they can have come from; such a corps then shows no needed slot below its minimum, and the line says so while it is open. On 1 December each Allied seat carries a switch to start on the map or hold off it, live for two corps; a corps that cannot start on the map says why in place of the switch: _two chosen_, or _too many units_ for its locale. Deploy is live once every unit is placed and the rules accept the set-up; until then the line beside it counts the units to assign and then gives the rule still broken.
- **Set-up view.** A set-up opens with the view on the locales the army sets up in, so every corps can be found before any seat is tapped. On a small screen (narrow, or as short as a phone on its side), opening a seat closes the view in on it: on a corps's set-up locale, or the locale the fixed battery stands in, and the locales around it; a seat with no one place on the map (a corps held off it, _Detached_, a fixed battery not yet placed) brings the whole set-up area back, which is also where a unit taken in hand finds the locales it may stand in. Taking a unit that stands on its own in hand by tapping it on the map moves nothing: the view stays where the tap was. The view is aimed at the part of the map that is on screen, because there the panel is tall and the lower part of the map is below the fold. On a wide screen the view does not zoom: it stays as it is, and only if it has been moved off the open corps's locale is that brought back in, at the zoom the board has. When the set-up is deployed the whole field comes back.
- **Printed set-up marks.** The map prints, on each corps's starting locale, a small stack of bars with the commander's name. They are of use only while the armies set up, so they are not part of the board art: they are a layer of their own, cut from the same map file and laid exactly over the art, shown for both players until both armies are deployed and gone once play begins (and shown again in History View on a position from the set-up). The objective stars and entry triangles printed beside them stay on the board throughout. The move replay never shows the marks.
- **Whole-field picking on small screens.** When pieces are picked up with the whole field in view, the view closes in on the pieces and the places they can go.
- **Header and action area.** One band across the top of the game column, set apart from the page by its ground alone, with no outline. It meets the page header above, and it and the board run to the window's right edge; only the side panel keeps a gap around it. In the band: a masthead line (which army's turn in italic, the time beside it at the same size, and Undo as plain italic text with an arrow standing on its baseline, never an outlined button; morale is not shown here but on the army cards in the side panel), a swelled rule with a lozenge, and under it the prompt and controls for the decision at hand. The masthead carries a light tint of the acting army's colour that fades out just below the rule; in History View and at the end of the game there is none. The masthead names the army whose turn it is (_Allies' turn_, _French turn_) for both players rather than _Your turn_, at the player's request: the tint and the army named already say whose it is, and both players read the same line. On a small screen the date beside the time is shortened (_1 Dec_). Two quick taps anywhere in the band are two taps, never a zoom: adding units in set-up and stepping a loss are tapped quickly, and a phone's double-tap zoom is switched off there (pinching and scrolling are not). Undo in the masthead is set at the size of the turn's title, on its baseline.
- **The map keeps half the viewport on a small screen.** The board is never shorter than half the viewport's height on a small screen and two fifths of it on a wider one, or the whole space under the masthead where that is less, and the part of it the orders sheet hangs over does not count towards that. When a panel is too tall for the map to fit under it, the panel and the map scroll together under the masthead, which stays where it is and takes no part in the scrolling; the scrolling stops dead at either end, with no spring. A new step opens scrolled to its prompt.
- **History.** The history panel files what happened under the round it happened in. An attack is one entry: the threat opens it and every step of the attack (the defence or retreat, the feint or the pressed attack, the counter-attack, losses, regrouping, the advance, the move into the locale) is listed under it, indented, as it happens, each with the stripe of the army that acted. The next threat opens an entry of its own. Ends of turns are left out. A line also says what morale an army paid beyond its losses, as the points its morale really fell: committing the Guard or the heavy cavalry where that happened (the declaration, the counter-attack, a loss, a retreat), and the defeat of a Guard Attack after the loser's losses.
- **History pictures.** No line of the history takes the game to another moment. Pressing a line whose action happened on the map opens a sheet over the table instead: the sentence of the action with its time, and under it the part of the map it concerned, turned as the board is turned, with every piece as it stood once the action was done. Pieces that moved stand where they went; the places they left, and any unit destroyed, are drawn as washed-out blocks where they stood, a corps that some units left keeping the shape it had so those places lie open beside it. A dark arrow with a light edge runs from each place left to where the pieces went, bending along the road they marched by and starting at the map edge for pieces that came on. A step of an attack carries the attack's own arrow across its approach and the letters of what each piece was doing (D, A, C, L); the defender stepping up to the approach afterwards, and the attacker moving into the locale it won, are shown by where they went, without the arrow. An attack called off as a feint has the same arrow, straight, turned back on the attacker, with the end of its shaft curled up like the end of a ribbon in a narrow band that shows the ribbon's darker other side; the board itself shows the same arrow while the defender answers the feint. Washing out always means one thing in a picture. Where pieces moved it marks only the places they left, and every unit still standing is drawn solid. Where nothing moved and the action names units (those that stand, attack, counter-attack, take a loss or stay with a commander), the units standing in the same stack as a named one are washed out, so the ones meant stand out; every other stack stays solid, and an action that names none, such as a threat, washes out nothing. An army being set out is shown where it was set down, with nothing moving. Lines for what happened off the map (the bids for the armies and the choice of one) open nothing and are not buttons. Above the sentence, beside _Close_, two arrowheads step the sheet to the pictured line before and the pictured line after in the order things happened, the steps of an attack following the attack they belong to; the left and right arrow keys do the same, and while the sheet is open the arrow keys belong to it and do not step the game through its history as they do on the table. They pass over lines with no picture, each is faint and does nothing at its end of the history, and they keep their place on the sheet from one picture to the next so they can be pressed again without looking. Stepping changes only the sheet. The sheet still offers _See the whole game at this point_, the one way from the history into History View, chosen on purpose and never by pressing a line.
- **History View.** The action area describes the action shown and nothing on the table can be picked. The map shows the position at that step and, during an attack, the attack marker and the roles that were public then.
- **Settling.** While the session is still settling an action or an Undo, the table takes no choice: pieces are not offered for picking up or naming, and the controls of the action area and the Undo button do not respond. Nothing changes appearance for it. What was picked before stays as it is until the new position arrives.

## Coexistence and precedence

- **A choice changes only itself.** This is a deliberate departure from the shared staged-selection rule that setting a stage clears the later ones, made at the player's request and carried by the two helpers `revise` and `withdraw`. Within one step, changing a choice never resets another that does not depend on it: the units chosen, the command and the Guard Attack announcement of an order; the command, width and leading units of an attack; which units advance and which commanders go with them; the command and the Guard Attack announcement of an order. Undo still takes the choices back one at a time. A retreat goes further and keeps its arrangement (which unit takes a loss, where each goes, whom a commander keeps) out of the staged selection altogether, as a set-up keeps its open seat: see _One Undo_.
- **One Undo.** The header's Undo is the only way back; no panel has a cancel or back control. An action that showed its player an enemy block they could not see (losses that fall on a hidden block, the deal of the armies after the auction, the end of the game) is not offered for Undo. Naming pieces is never secret; only a block turned face-up is. Pressing an attack shows nothing, so the attacker can take it back for as long as the defender has not answered. Once the defender's naming of the leading units has turned one face-up, neither that naming nor the press before it can be undone; naming no leading unit shows nothing and can be taken back. Pressing the choice already in force on a switch, or the leading unit already struck, is no choice and adds no step. While deploying it steps back through the changes to the army only (a unit placed or taken back, an approach blocked, a corps put on or held off the map); which seat is open and which unit is in hand are not steps, at the player's request, though they are the player's own taps. It steps back through the picks of the attack step in front of the player during an attack, and through the command being built otherwise, one manual choice at a time; with none left it undoes the last committed action. Choices the table makes by itself (the units a pick-up starts with, the riding-on selection, the attackers taken from the threat, the default retreat) are never a step of their own. Nor, at the player's request, is anything moved about while a retreat is arranged: a step of a loss moved onto another unit, a unit sent to a locale, a commander moved to another unit. Each is redone by another tap. In a retreat Undo answers only to what is settled and to what is held: with a unit or a flag picked up it puts it down; with nothing held it goes back from where the units go to the losses, dropping where they were sent; and from the losses it goes back to what came before the retreat.
- Within an attack step Undo takes the latest stage first: a retreat being arranged is unwound before the choice to retreat, and that before the defenders picked earlier, which are still there when the player comes back to them.
- Picking up pieces is possible only in the command phase; an attack in progress replaces it, so the selection ring, the orders in the action area and move targets never coexist with battle picking.
- Retreat planning takes precedence over naming pieces: while a retreat is being arranged, blocks pick a unit to send by itself and locales are retreat targets.
- Riding on yields to any pick-up by the player, of another group or of another unit in the riders' own group; while nothing else is picked up it takes the place of the empty selection.
- Choosing the road replaces the marks of the places to go with the marks of the roads to one place; Undo, or changing the units or the command, brings the places back. The legend lists its marks as marches by road.
- Battle focus and whole-field picking cannot overlap: the second happens only in the command phase.
- Dimming for "left out of the command" applies only to the picked-up group; dimming for "no part in this attack" applies to every piece while an attack is on. The two never apply together.
- The army as arranged in set-up replaces the live position on the map only for the deploying player and only until the set-up is committed or the taps are undone.
- The move replay is a modal sheet over a table that stays as it is: it never changes the position shown, the selection, the view of the board or what Undo would do, and it can be opened in the present or from within History View. Only one is open at a time. New actions arriving underneath leave it open; it closes only when the action it shows is no longer in the history shown.
- History View and settling both withhold every choice; History View also hides Undo and replaces the action area's content.

## Render ownership

- **Dimming, picking, roles and moved marks of a block** come from the block marks alone. The pieces layer is their only renderer on the map. The battle panel's summary of forces shows only the roles the units were named to in the fight, which the session gives it apart from the picks of the step now open, so stepping up or regrouping never re-marks a unit listed there; the map shows the step's picks over those roles, a retreat being arranged first. And the orders' unit strip takes its moved dimming from the same rule; neither works out a role or a dimming of its own.
- **Dimming is a change of paint, never of opacity.** A dimmed block, on the map or on a tile, is drawn solid in a washed-out tone of its army's colour with its symbols toned to match, so the map never shows through a piece and the two armies stay distinguishable.
- **Places to go** are one list. The target layer draws it twice around the pieces: locales and approaches to block underneath, so a block always takes the tap where the two overlap, and threat arrows on top, because they sit on approaches where blocks stand, together with the arrow signs of a road choice, which must stay in reach where stacks cover the locale. A threat arrow has the attack marker's shape and size, in ink where the marker is red, and turns red under the pointer.
- **Arrows are never covered.** Threat arrows, the attack marker and retreat arrows are the top layers of the board, over the flags; the attack marker and retreat arrows take no taps. A threat arrow hands the session the board point it was tapped at, and the session decides whether a block of the chosen group lies under it; the layer does no hit-testing of its own.
- **Selection ring** belongs to the pieces layer, over the blocks. **Commander flags** are drawn over the pieces and the approach symbols but under every arrow, and keep clear of the arrows where they can. Neither takes taps; a group is picked up by its blocks. Where a flag stands and which way it flies is decided once, in the session, from every block and flag on the board; the flag component only draws it.
- **Held-approach tints and track markers** (the command discs and the morale and time markers, drawn by one layer as the same disc) are the lowest layers on the board art, under the places to go and the pieces, and take no taps. The markers' positions are measured from the printed tracks, so they must be measured again if the board art changes.
- **Set-up locales** are a layer of their own, above the held-approach tints and under the places to go, so a marked locale or approach takes the tap where they overlap. They tint the set-up locale of each corps that starts on the map and take the tap that opens it. The flag of a corps with no unit yet is planted by the flag layer at that locale's stack point, and is placed with the other flags so none covers another.
- **Locale shapes**, which mark the places to go and take their taps, are built from the printed bars of the public map file: each side lies on the centre line of its bar, and where the map prints no bar the shape ends at the edge of the battlefield. The bars meeting at a junction do not all pass through one point, so each shape stops where its sides reach the small patch in which those lines cross one another; that patch belongs to no locale, and neighbouring shapes do not overlap. Reserve stacks stand at the roomiest point of their locale.
- **Movement trails** are a layer of their own between the pieces and the approach symbols. The session works out their lines; the layer only draws them, with the arrow the history pictures use.
- **Approach symbols** are drawn over the pieces and under the attack marker and flags, and take no taps. They and the border tint come from the bars and marks of the public map file, not from the rules data, so they show what is printed.
- **The colours of the places to go** are named values set once on the table and used by both the target layer and the legend, so the legend always shows the map's own marks.
- **The attack arrow's shape** is defined once and used both to draw the marker and to keep flags off it.
- **The move replay** draws its own small map from the same parts as the board (the piece layout, the block, the contact shadow, the flag placement, the held-approach and border-tint layers), so a piece looks the same in both. The attack's arrow, straight or folded back for a feint, is one drawing used by the board's marker and by the picture. Where the pieces stood before and after the action is read from the recorded history with the shared recorded-history reader, which never moves the history cursor and never touches the game. The session works the scene out from those two positions and the action's own record in one place (who stands where, the places left, each arrow with its shaft and head, the points flags must keep off, the frame); the sheet measures only its own width, to size the flags, and draws. An arrow's shaft ends inside its head, both are edged before either is filled, and a line too short for a full head gets a smaller arrow rather than a head with no shaft. Nothing in the sheet takes a tap except its two controls.
- **The saved picture** is put together in the browser from what is already drawn. The board marks the layers that belong in it (track markers and held approaches, pieces, flags); the picture copies those as they stand, without the selection ring, into a drawing that carries its own fonts and shadow filter, and lays it over the board art on a canvas turned as the board is. No layer is drawn a second time for it, so a piece in the picture is the piece on the board. For a picture without unit types the session covers the faces on the map (the blocks', nowhere else) only for as long as the copy takes, within one turn of the page, so nothing is painted in between. The art and the fonts are read again from where the game serves them, because a picture can only be read back out of a canvas whose images the page was allowed to read.
- **Empty map** puts pieces down. Blocks and targets keep their own taps from reaching it.
- **Board turning** is one transform on the board. The piece layout decides every block's angle and drawing order: reserve stacks are laid out for the turned board so they stay readable, blocking pieces by their approach alone. Flags, count badges and retreat counts turn back to stay upright.
- **The board is drawn at the scale it is shown.** The shared scaling wrapper lays the board out again at the view's scale once the view rests and stretches it only while the view is moving, so the board costs what is on screen. Marks that follow the zoom read the wrapper's scale; nothing measures the board on screen.
- **The contact shadow** is cast by each stack and each track marker on its own, on the board and in the move replay. A shadow over a whole layer of pieces would blur an area the size of the board on every repaint.
- **Moving the view** belongs to the table alone, through the shared scaling wrapper: battle focus, its return, the set-up view, whole-field picking, finding a corps, and bringing picked-up pieces out from under the orders sheet. The map menu only asks for the board to be turned to a view. A place the view was sent to is kept in front of the player while the map area changes size, until the player moves the view by hand, whether by mouse, wheel, pinch or one finger; after that the view stays where the player left it. Nothing watches state to move the view: the table hears of an attack beginning or ending from the session's state-change listener, which is how it tells a played action from Undo or a history step, and it is told by the session when pieces are picked up. It measures the orders sheet itself at that moment. No layer pans or zooms.
- **The action area** owns every prompt and control, the orders for chosen pieces included. With nothing chosen the command phase is one sentence, "Choose a corps or unit on the map or [End turn]", naming any corps that can be brought on. With pieces chosen their name takes that line and a sheet hangs from the band over the top of the board: the unit strip, then the commands on offer with End turn at the right, then guidance. The band itself keeps its height, so choosing never resizes the board, and the scaling wrapper keeps focused views clear of the sheet by its measured height; the table moves the board only when the pieces just picked up would lie under the sheet. The command switch sits under a small "Command" label, as in an attack. Nothing else but the map menu lies over the board.
- **Colours of the action area** are a small set of named values (ground, ink, warning, hover) set once on the table and used by every panel, button and unit tile inside it, so the area can change ground without any panel knowing.
- **Acting for a player needs their army in view.** The session offers no choices, set-up included, while the state on hand hides the faces of the acting player's own units. That happens for a moment when an administrator switches Admin mode on in a game they are not seated in as that player: the acting player is named at once and the full state follows.
- **Withholding choices while settling** is decided in the session for the map and, for the controls of the masthead and of the action area, by a disabled group around each that follows the same state. Styles that show a control as unavailable follow the control's own state, so a settling table does not flicker.
